ART DIRECTION
=============

Status: rewritten from scratch 2026-08-16, against the Cast n Chill frames in
resources/images/. This is the second version of this file. The first was
written for V5 and deleted in commit e05609d without anything replacing it,
while roughly fifteen places in the code, the tools and the roadmap went on
citing it by name for a specific rule. Those citations are what this rewrite is
answerable to: every section below that a caller names is marked with who names
it, so the next person to delete a section can see what breaks.

This document is the thing every V item is checked against. If a decision here
and a decision in an asset disagree, this file is wrong or the asset is; one of
them gets changed, and the losing one gets a dated line here saying so.

**Superseding note, and it is a correction rather than a tidy-up.** ROADMAP.md's
V5 entry closes with "the crossing stills are retired, the Noita-forest
screenshot is adopted as the first quantum world's direction." The Noita
screenshot no longer exists in resources/ and no observation of it survives, so
that resolution is unverifiable from here. This pass adopts the Cast n Chill
frames instead. The crossing stills stay retired — that half of the resolution
is unchanged and was right. A dated correction is filed beside the V5 entry
rather than over it, per .claude/rules/documentation.md.


1. WHICH LOCATIONS EXIST, AND WHAT EACH ONE READS AS
----------------------------------------------------

The fiction (notes/story.txt) is a dystopian United States run by a chatbot,
with "quantum magic" as the way through to other worlds. That gives two kinds of
place, and the plan has always budgeted for the second one badly.

  HOME BASE — the dystopian US. Not built, not authored, not scheduled. It is
  the frame story and it is where the player returns. Named here so that the
  first asset authored for it has to come back and add a section, rather than
  quietly inheriting the palette below.

  QUANTUM WORLDS — the places the player is dropped into and digs through. One
  exists: the test fixture's world, which today reads as a warm near-black
  earth section under a cool night sky. That is the location this whole
  document's palette describes.

  Locations are per-world from here on. A second biome gets its own section
  when it exists; it does not inherit this one's palette by default. This was
  V5's rule and it survives the rewrite unchanged.

The first quantum world reads as: **night, underground-adjacent, warm ground
under a cold sky.** Not snow, not a level crossing. The player is outdoors but
the interesting half of the frame is the earth, because the earth is the part
that simulates.


2. THE PALETTE'S INTENT
-----------------------

Named by: tools/pixel_art.py (the PALETTE dict is grouped by this document's
layer model), tools/export_palette_gpl.py.

The rule, unchanged from V2 and now stated as a rule rather than as a property
of one table:

  - **The backdrop is cool and carries no warm colour at all.** Zero. This is
    what keeps it reading as *behind* rather than as far-away-but-present.
  - **The simulated world is warm and desaturated.** Warm near-black rather
    than cool near-black, so unlit dirt reads as earth and not as void.
  - **Saturation is reserved for Fire and Water.** They are the two materials
    that must be identifiable at a glance in a frame where everything is
    moving, and they get the only saturation budget in the set.
  - **Water is the deliberate exception to "desaturated".** It keeps more
    saturation than anything else in the terrain layer because it has to read
    as water up close, not only in silhouette.
  - **The warm-orange family belongs to Fire.** Anything else warm-orange is
    borrowing, and has to say so at the point it borrows. `char_accent` is the
    one current borrower and pixel_art.py carries the argument for it.

**A finding from this pass that the palette does not currently satisfy, and it
is the most useful thing the reference produced.** Measured off the CnC frames
(method and numbers in notes/reference_observations.txt), depth is carried by
*band value*, and it is carried differently by day and by night:

  - By day, the bands descend monotonically in both value and saturation from
    sky to foreground — aerial perspective. Far things are brighter and more
    saturated because they are closer to the sky's colour.
  - By night, that ordering breaks and is replaced by silhouette: the sky stays
    the brightest thing in frame, the nearest shapes go to near-black and read
    as outline only, and the readable detail lives in the middle band.

Our palette does neither. Its bands overlap almost completely — sky spans
roughly 0x14–0x28, mountains 0x20–0x30, trees 0x18–0x59, terrain 0x1B–0x78 —
so **nothing separates one depth band from the next except the rim highlight.**
That is a real defect and it is why a busy frame here reads as flat. Fixing it
needs a per-band exposure the renderer cannot currently express, because the
light pass can only add (V11); it is not a palette edit and must not be
attempted as one. Recorded here rather than acted on.


3. THE DITHERING RULE
---------------------

Named by: tools/pixel_art.py ("the dithering rule"), tools/snap_to_palette.py,
and apply_rim_light's dithered hand-off band.

  **A transition between two flat tones is stepped through an ordered dither.
  Never a smooth interpolation, never a hard edge with nothing between.**

The matrix is the classic 4x4 Bayer, in `pixel_art.bayer_threshold`. The pick
between two tones is a per-pixel coin flip whose outcomes land in that grid —
`dither_mix` — and it returns one of the two input colours, never a third. That
last part is the point: a blend would produce a colour no editor's palette
contains, and V6's validator would reject it.

**Random per-cell jitter stays out of everything this pipeline authors.** V2
found the negative case: jitter fights hand-placed dither instead of adding to
it, which is exactly why `color_jitter` was cut. Do not reintroduce it as a
"cheap texture" pass.

**Dithering is authored, not computed.** snap_to_palette.py gets pixels
on-palette and deliberately does not add dither; that hand-off is a manual pass
in a sprite editor. The one place the pipeline dithers on its own is the rim's
hand-off band, below, and that one is called out by name because it is the
exception.

The reference supports this at a specific scale: the CnC frames dither the sky
gradient and the water heavily, and leave large filled masses almost untextured.
Dither is for *transitions*, not for surfaces.


4. WHAT THE PLAYER READS AS
---------------------------

Named by: tools/snap_to_palette.py (the char_* restriction), tools/build_player_
sheet.py, tools/pixel_art.py's player group.

The body is **8 wide by 20 tall, in cells** — Noita's proportions, and the
authority is `Player::WIDTH` / `Player::HEIGHT` in src/physics/player.h, not
this file. *(The first version of this document said 4x8 in several places and
was stale for a long time after the rescale. Quoted constants go stale exactly
the way line numbers do, and are harder to spot because they read as
deliberate. If this pair and player.h ever disagree, player.h is right.)*

A hooded figure in a robe, with a mask. Read at 8x20 that is: one silhouette,
one hood shape, and about six pixels of anything else.

**The separation problem, and the rule that solves it.** Every other group in
the palette is either cool-and-dark (sky, mountains) or warm-and-dark (terrain),
with the trees between them in green. A character painted in any of those
families disappears into whichever one it happens to be standing against — and
in this game the background is *simulated*, so it changes underneath the figure
constantly and cannot be composed around.

  **So the player is separated from its background by HUE, not by value.** The
  robe is cool blue-grey: the one hue family the terrain layer never uses. It
  reads against warm dirt and against green foliage without being brighter than
  either, and its value range stays inside the locked set's — `char_light` tops
  out at 0x5E, below `tree_lit`'s 0x59 top end by a hair.

This is why `snap_to_palette.py` defaults to searching only the six `char_*`
entries. A global nearest-colour search would happily paint a highlight onto the
nearest tree tone and undo the one thing keeping the figure legible. Restricting
the candidate set makes that structurally impossible rather than merely
discouraged.

*(V5 left this section unanswered and V3 was authored afterwards. This rewrite
records what V3 actually shipped rather than what V5 planned; the sprite is the
authority and this is the description.)*


5. WHAT IS DRAWN VERSUS WHAT IS SIMULATED — THE FOUR-LAYER MODEL
-----------------------------------------------------------------

Named by: ASSETS.md ("the four-layer scenery model"), src/main.cpp in two
places, tools/generate_backdrop.py ("layer 1 and 2"), tools/generate_props.py,
tools/pixel_art.py (PALETTE is grouped by these layers), generate_test_scene.py.
This is the most-cited section in the file.

  **The test: if it exercises no system, it belongs in a drawn layer.**

Not "if it is far away" and not "if it is decoration" — if the simulation would
learn nothing from it, it is art, and putting it in the grid costs the hot loop
for nothing.

The four scenery layers, back to front:

  1. SKY        — drawn. One BMP. Cool, deep, no warm colour.
  2. MOUNTAINS  — drawn. One BMP, colour-keyed. Flat silhouette plus a faint
                  peak rim.
  3. PROPS      — drawn. Colour-keyed sprites anchored to world positions;
                  trees today. Never dug, ignited or displaced.
  4. TERRAIN    — **simulated.** The cell grid. This is the game.

**The player and the light pass are not layers in this model.** They sit on top
of it. The full render order — which ASSETS.md also states, and which is the
thing to change if a band is added — is:

    sky -> mountains -> props -> terrain -> player -> light -> reticle -> HUD

**Props draw BEFORE terrain, and this is the ordering that surprises people.**
The obvious arrangement is props-on-top, and it is wrong here. Drawing a trunk
before the cell texture means authored ground *buries* the trunk's base for
free, with no depth test and no per-prop mask. A planted tree gets its occlusion
from the draw order alone. Anything that wants the same trick goes in the same
slot.

**Effects that expire — sparks, embers, dust, smoke wisps — are drawn, on the
props layer.** They interact with nothing, so simulating them buys nothing.
Do not confuse this with E5a: E5a is matter with a velocity that stays in the
grid and conserves. A spark that has to conserve mass is a cell and belongs in
layer 4.

**Backdrop motion is drawn and interacts with nothing.** A moving thing a player
could reasonably expect to touch has failed this test. Weather that lands on
terrain is a simulation feature and is a different item.


6. THE DEPTH-BAND MODEL
-----------------------

New in this rewrite. There was no written home for parallax factors, so
main.cpp and tools/generate_backdrop.py each kept their own copy of the numbers
with a comment asking a human to keep them in sync.

*(Updated 2026-08-16, V11. The duplication is gone: tools/generate_backdrop.py
is the one copy and generates src/render/backdrop_layers.h from it. This
section is still where the numbers are *argued*; the header is where they are
*stated*, and the two must not drift — if you retune, the row below moves in
the same commit as the regenerated header.)*

Bands, with the factors as they ship today:

    band        factor x   factor y   contents
    sky            0.04       0.02     layer 1
    mountains      0.15       0.06     layer 2
    props/world    1.00       1.00     layers 3 and 4, locked to the camera

Two rules about these numbers:

  - **Layers 3 and 4 are at exactly 1.0 and that is not a tuning choice.** A
    prop is anchored to a world cell and the terrain *is* world cells; a factor
    other than 1.0 would put a tree's roots somewhere the ground is not. Only
    bands 1 and 2 have a factor to tune.

  - **The factors were chosen by eye and have never been measured.** In
    particular they were *not* derived from the CnC frames — that was attempted
    on 2026-08-16 and the attempt failed for a reason worth knowing, written up
    in notes/reference_observations.txt. Treat them as a starting point with no
    evidence behind them, which is a different thing from a tuned value.

**A mid-ground band was built on 2026-08-16 and removed the same day, and this
is the paragraph that used to argue for one.** It said the stack jumps from
mountains at 0.15 straight to the world at 1.00, that the reference frames all
carry a distinct band in between doing most of the depth work, and that there was
no slot for one because the layer order was hard-coded inline. V11 made the slot
(it is an ordered table in src/render/frame.cpp now, so a band is one row), put a
band in it at 0.40/0.16, and then ran the check that
notes/reference_observations.txt entry 4 had written against itself.

**The check said our terrain already fills that band**, and the entry is marked
disproved with the full argument. The short version for this document, because it
is a fact about our art direction and not just about that note: **the reference
paints a mid-ground because a painting has nothing else to put there. We
simulate one.** Our world is 1080 cells tall and the camera sees 270 of them, so
the space between the player and the horizon is 800 cells of real ground. A band
drawn into it would be a band behind terrain.

Two further reasons it stays out, both of which would have applied even if the
gap had been there, and the first is the one that matters to section 8's held-open
questions:

  - **A band authored today lands in exactly the overlap it exists to break.**
    Entry 2 measured our bands at sky 0x14–0x28, mountains 0x20–0x30, trees
    0x18–0x59, terrain 0x1B–0x78 — near-total overlap, which is why a busy frame
    reads flat. Any mid-ground drawn from the existing palette groups sits in
    the same range as the mountains behind it. Separating them is a multiply and
    the light pass can only add, so it is the V block's step 3 and not a palette
    edit. **Depth here is a renderer problem before it is a layer-count problem**,
    and adding bands is the wrong lever for it — which is the real lesson, since
    a band was very nearly the answer to a question it could not have answered.

  - **It would be the most expensive layer in the stack, not the cheapest.**
    Layer size grows with parallax factor, so at 0.40 the BMP is 5750x1965 —
    32 MB, more than the sky and mountains together, in a repo that tracks its
    assets. V16's wrapping layers retire the size relationship entirely.

*Reopen trigger:* a location whose terrain does not fill the band — a flatter
scene with a lower horizon than F4's snowbank, or a zoomed-out camera once
`Camera::SCALE` is runtime.


7. THE RIM
----------

Named by: ENGINEERING_NOTES.md (the cell-size entry, "measured in
art_direction.txt's rim section"), tests/rim_probe.cpp, generate_test_scene.py
(twice), tools/pixel_art.py's apply_rim_light.

  **A filled region reads as shadowed mass with almost no internal texture, and
  the one to two cells facing open air carry a bright, warm highlight.**

That is the whole technique, and it is what carries shape in a palette this
dark. It comes from the reference directly: the CnC frames leave large masses
nearly flat and spend their contrast budget on edges.

Three consequences, each of which has already cost something:

  **The rim is a proportion of the body, not a pixel count.** Two cells against
  a 20-cell player is the thin bright line the reference has. The same two cells
  against the old 8-cell body was a quarter of its height. When the scale
  changes, `rim_depth` changes with it — generate_test_scene.py passes `s(2)`,
  scaled by the same factor as everything else in the fixture, for this reason.

  **The hand-off inward is dithered, not hard.** The rim colour steps toward
  whatever albedo was already there across the remaining `rim_depth - 1` cells,
  through the Bayer matrix. This is the one case where the pipeline dithers on
  its own, and section 3 calls it out as the exception.

  **The rim is baked per cell, so a lit top edge travels with the cell.** This
  is accepted, deliberately, and it is the price of authored per-cell colour —
  which is the entire visual pillar, because it is what lets a hand-painted slab
  keep its own pixels while it collapses. At this scale a travelled highlight
  reads as material texture. It only breaks down under large-scale gradients,
  which is why **none get painted into the simulated layer.**

  That acceptance is not a guess: tests/rim_probe.cpp measures how much of the
  rim survives settling, in two numbers — SMEAR (rim cells that no longer face
  air) and GAPS (surface cells with no rim). It is not an `add_test`, because
  what it reports is an input to a taste decision and a test pinning today's
  taste would be a copy of the answer rather than a check on it. Wall and Wood
  never move and are fine by construction; Sand and Water are the ones to read.

Rim colour is per material, not global: water gets a cooler rim than ground,
and a material that should stay unlit at its surface returns None and is
skipped.


8. WHAT THIS PASS DOES NOT ANSWER
----------------------------------

Held open on purpose, so that the next thing to need one of these knows it is
choosing rather than following:

  - **The home base has no direction.** Section 1 names it and stops.
  - **Time of day is one setting: night.** The reference shows day, dusk and
    night, and section 2's measurement says the depth cueing has to change
    between them. Nothing here says what dusk looks like in our palette.
  - **The band-value defect in section 2 is recorded, not fixed**, and cannot
    be fixed by editing colours.
  - **No second biome.** The moment one exists it gets its own section 1 entry
    and its own palette group, and does not inherit this one's.
