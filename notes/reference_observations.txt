REFERENCE OBSERVATIONS
======================

Status: rewritten from scratch 2026-08-16. The first version was written for V5
and deleted in commit e05609d; several places in the tools and the roadmap kept
citing it for specific conclusions, and those conclusions are re-derived here
against the frames actually present rather than reconstructed from the
citations. Where a citation's claim could not be reproduced, that is said.

**The frames are gitignored and the written observation is the artifact.**
resources/ is ignored wholesale (.gitignore has the argument), so nothing below
can be checked by opening the image on another machine, and nothing in the asset
pipeline ever reads a pixel out of one. That is deliberate and it is what keeps
"our direction informed by theirs" from sliding into "their palette lifted."

**What this file is for, and the bound on it.** Reference answers "what is
possible" and never "what is wrong here". It cannot admit a roadmap item. The
legitimate uses are exactly three, and they are the three V5 named:

    - contrast and readability against a busy simulated background
    - how much of a frame is not simulated at all
    - what the eye is drawn to when everything is moving

An observation that does not serve one of those is breadth spending wearing
engine clothes. If reading this file makes you want to build something, that
want is not evidence.

ENTRY FORMAT: source, date observed, what was measured or seen, and — the part
that makes it useful later — what it would take to disprove it. An observation
with no disproof condition is an impression.


-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SOURCE: Cast n Chill — eight frames, resources/images/CnC_*.jpg
Observed 2026-08-16.
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A fishing game. Painted pixel-art lakes, a boat, a dog, a rod. Chosen as
reference for three things it does that we want and one thing it does that we
had assumed wrongly.

**How much of the frame is simulated: essentially none of it.** This is the
first and most important thing about using it as reference. The lake is a
painting. The only things that move are the boat, the fish, the water shimmer
and the weather. Every depth cue below is therefore *authored per frame* by a
human who knew where everything would be — and we cannot do that, because our
background is the game. Anything taken from these frames has to survive being
composed by a simulation instead of by an artist. That constraint is not
visible in the frames and has to be carried in from here every time.


ENTRY 1 — the three "parallax" frames are not one scene panning, and the
          attempt to measure parallax factors from them failed.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_parallax_1.jpg, _2.jpg, _3.jpg (3440x1440 each).

These were taken to be the same lake at three camera offsets, which would have
made them a free measurement of per-layer parallax factors — exactly the number
our own bands (art_direction.txt section 6) have never had.

Measured: per-region best horizontal shift by sum-of-absolute-differences over
candidate offsets, on hand-picked regions each belonging to one depth layer
(band-wise measurement is useless here — a horizontal band across this scene
holds the far range on one side and the near shore on the other, and the best
single shift for that mixture means nothing). Frames 1 -> 3, confidence measured
as how far the best candidate beats the median candidate:

    far snow range, right         +200 px    95%
    flat-top red cliff, centre     +56 px    84%
    near dark mountain, left       +96 px    88%
    conifer, right shore          -260 px    62%

**Two of these are impossible for a single camera pan.** The far range shifts
*more* than the near mountain, which inverts parallax; and the left and right
edges shift in opposite directions, which no translation produces. A 2D search
was run in case vertical camera motion was contaminating an x-only match against
sloped silhouettes — dy came back 0 in every region, so that is not it.

**Conclusion: these are three separately generated lakes sharing an art
vocabulary, not one scene panning.** The same flat-top cliff sprite appears in
all three at different x, which is what made them look like a pan. Frame 3 has a
large deciduous tree at the right edge with no counterpart at any offset in the
others.

**So: no parallax factors can be taken from this reference.** Our 0.04/0.15
were chosen by eye and remain unmeasured. Writing a "measured" factor into the
tuning table off these frames would have been fabrication, and it was within one
step of happening.

*Disproof:* find a genuine pan — two frames with a shared, unambiguous near
feature — and the region shifts should then be monotonic in depth and agree in
sign. Video capture would settle it in seconds; stills cannot.


ENTRY 2 — depth is carried by band VALUE, and the ordering flips between day
*(2026-08-22: entry 11 measures a daylight set and finds this half right —
the far half of a day frame does descend monotonically, the near half is unlit
framing and is not on the ramp at all.)*
          and night. This is the palette-sample note.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_parallax_1.jpg (day), CnC_night.jpg, CnC_fishing.jpg (night).

Measured: mean of max(r,g,b), mean HLS saturation, and median hue over
hand-picked regions, one per depth band.

  CnC_parallax_1 (day):
    band                V mean    S mean   hue
    sky                  207       0.37    211
    far range            185       0.26    220
    mid mountains        130       0.23    218
    treeline             105       0.20    200
    foreground rock      103       0.16    232

  CnC_night (night):
    sky                  163       0.42    217
    far range             58       0.51    219
    mid forest           123       0.23    208
    foreground rock      141       0.34    210
    near conifer          36       0.57    160

**By day the bands descend monotonically in both value and saturation from sky
to foreground.** That is aerial perspective: far things sit closer to the sky's
colour, near things are darker and greyer. Hue barely moves — 200 to 232 across
the whole frame, all of it blue. The separation is done entirely by value and
saturation with a near-constant hue.

**By night that ordering breaks and is replaced by silhouette.** The sky stays
the brightest thing in frame; the nearest shapes drop to near-black (the conifer
at V 36) and read as outline only; and the readable detail moves into the middle
band, which is the *brightest* band after the sky. The far range goes dark
rather than pale — the opposite of the day frame.

**The thing this is worth to us.** Our palette does neither, and its bands
overlap almost completely: sky roughly 0x14–0x28, mountains 0x20–0x30, trees
0x18–0x59, terrain 0x1B–0x78. Nothing separates one band from the next except
the rim highlight, which is why a busy frame here reads as flat. This is the
"contrast against a busy simulated background" question, answered, and the
answer is that we are not currently answering it.

It is also a *renderer* finding rather than a palette one. Pushing a band's
value range down globally is a multiply, and the light pass can only add. So
this cannot be fixed by editing colours, and must not be attempted as a palette
change.

*** ACTED ON 2026-08-16 (V11 step 3), AND THE MEASUREMENT WAS WORSE THAN THIS
*** ENTRY MADE IT LOOK.
The channel ranges above understate it, because a range is not a separation.
Re-measured in Rec. 709 luminance over the actual shipped BMPs: sky mean 26
(p05 18, p95 35), mountains **flat 28** — p05 and p95 both 28, no internal
variation whatsoever — trees mean 42, terrain 38 to 171. So the two most distant
bands in the frame are separated by two levels out of 255, and the mountains are
the *brighter* of the pair, which is the wrong sign for a night scene as well as
the wrong magnitude.

The renderer half of the claim held exactly as written, and it is the reason the
fix took a week rather than an afternoon: there was no number anywhere in the
project that could darken a pixel. `render/frame.cpp`'s layer table now carries a
per-layer `Grade`, and the mountains sit at 0.60 — the pair reads 26 against 16
and the band is a silhouette. TUNING.md carries the row.

**One correction to the reasoning above, not to the finding.** This entry says
"pushing a band's value range down *globally*", and global is what the fix is
not. A single frame-wide multiply cannot separate two bands: it scales both and
leaves the ratio between them where it was. Separation is necessarily per-layer.
The world-wide grade got built too, and it is a different feature — night and
weather — which is worth keeping distinct, because reading this entry as an
argument for the global knob would be reading it as an argument for the one knob
that could not have answered it.

*Still open:* trees at 42 sit inside the terrain's 38-171 and are not separated
from it by anything. The rim highlight is what carries that boundary today (entry
3), which is a narrower instrument than a value step. Not touched at step 3
because trees are drawn *behind* the cell texture and are occluded by it, so
what is actually visible of a tree is the part standing against the sky — where
it does separate. Worth re-measuring on a frame with a tree against open ground.

*Disproof:* sample more regions per band, or regions chosen by someone else. The
day frame's monotonic descent is robust — five bands, one direction, no
exceptions — and is the half worth trusting. The night frame's non-monotonic
ordering rests on five hand-picked regions in one image and could be an artefact
of which rocks were sampled.

*Re-measured at greater depth in ENTRY 7 on 2026-08-16*, in luminance and down a
single column rather than over hand-picked regions. That pass finds **seven**
bands in CnC_parallax_1 rather than five, puts a number on the step between them
(about 0.78 per band), and disagrees with the table above on one figure — the
foreground rock. Read entry 7 for the ladder; this entry is still the one that
carries the day-versus-night ordering flip, which entry 7 does not touch.

*(This is the "dated entry" and the "palette-sample note" that tools/pixel_art.py
and generate_test_scene.py cite. Both cited the deleted version; what they
describe — dark, narrow value range, cool sky over warm ground, and flat
interiors being the point — is consistent with what is measured above, so those
comments are left as they stand.)*


ENTRY 3 — the rim technique: flat masses, contrast spent on edges.
*(2026-08-22: entry 11 scopes this to masses in shadow; the reference's lit
masses carry 5-20x the tone count. The reading is correct as taken, the scope
was not.)*
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: all eight, most clearly CnC_night.jpg and CnC_parallax_1.jpg.

Seen rather than measured. Large filled regions — rock, forest mass, mountain
flank — carry almost no internal texture. They are close to a single tone. What
carries the shape is the boundary: one to two pixels along the edge facing the
light or facing open air, held bright and warm against the flat mass behind it.

The value range *within* a mass is small; the jump *across* an edge is large.
That is where the whole contrast budget goes.

**This is the technique tools/pixel_art.py's `apply_rim_light` implements**, and
it is the one that made a dark palette legible at all. It is written up as a
rule, with its three consequences, in art_direction.txt's rim section — which is
where it belongs, because it is now our rule rather than an observation.

*Disproof:* a frame where a large mass carries real internal texture and still
reads. The lighthouse frame's clouds come closest — they are heavily modelled
internally — but clouds are the exception the frames themselves make, and they
are sky, not mass.


ENTRY 4 — a mid-ground band is doing most of the depth work, and we have no
          slot for one.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_parallax_1/2/3, CnC_night.jpg, CnC_fishing.jpg.

Every one of these frames carries a distinct band between "distant mountains"
and "the ground the character is on": a treeline, a near ridge, a bank of rocks.
It is the band with the most silhouette detail and it is the one that sells the
distance, because the mountains are too far to move and the foreground is too
close to read as anything but where you are.

Our stack goes mountains at 0.15 straight to the world at 1.00. That is a very
large gap with nothing in it, and there is no place to put a band because the
layer order is hard-coded inline in main.cpp.

*(This is the conclusion ROADMAP.md's V11 and ROADMAP_ITEMS.md both cite this
file for, and it is re-derived here rather than inherited: the deleted version's
wording is gone, but the frames still say it, in five of eight.)*

*Disproof:* it is a composition observation from a game that composes by hand.
A simulated world might fill that band with terrain by itself — our world is
1080 cells tall and the camera sees 270 of them, so there is real ground
between the player and the horizon. Worth checking against a played frame
before building a band that terrain was already occupying.

*** DISPROVED 2026-08-16, by the check named above, on the same day the entry
*** was written.

V11 built the band — a slot at parallax 0.40 between the mountains and the
world — and then the played-frame check went out as manual-checklist step 11:
standing on the surface, looking at the horizon, is there a visible gap between
the mountains and the ground you are on? The answer came back **no: the terrain
already fills it.** The layer was removed the same day, and the golden checksum
returned to exactly the value it had before V11 touched anything.

**The observation above is correct about the reference and wrong about us**, and
those are different claims that this file exists to keep apart. Five of eight
CnC frames really do carry that band and it really is doing most of their depth
work. What does not transfer is the reason: they compose by hand and *have* to
author that band, because nothing else will put anything there. We simulate it.
The gap the reference fills with a painted treeline, we fill with 800 cells of
actual ground.

**This is the file's opening constraint firing, and it is worth naming as such.**
The header of this document says every depth cue in these frames is authored per
frame by a human who knew where everything would be, that we cannot do that
because our background is the game, and that "anything taken from these frames
has to survive being composed by a simulation instead of by an artist." Entry 4
is the first observation to fail exactly that test — not by being a bad
observation, but by being one whose *mechanism* was hand-composition. The
constraint was written down in advance, at the top of this file, and it still
took building the band to notice it applied here.

**The generalisable form, for the next entry that looks like this one: ask what
in the reference is doing the work, not just what the work is.** "A mid-ground
band carries the depth" is a description of a result. "A human paints one there
because nothing else would be" is the mechanism, and the mechanism is the half
that tells you whether it transfers.

*Reopen trigger, so this is a closed loop and not a dead end:* a location whose
terrain does **not** fill that band. F4's fixture scene has a snowbank and a high
horizon; a flatter scene with a low horizon would restore the gap, and so would
a zoomed-out camera once `Camera::SCALE` becomes a runtime value (V11's remaining
bullet). Either of those and the band is worth building again — the layer table
in src/render/frame.cpp makes it one row, which is the thing V11 actually bought.


ENTRY 5 — the split view: what it actually is, and what it is not.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_fishing.jpg, CnC_underwater_1.jpg, CnC_underwater_2.jpg.

This is the frame family that prompted the split-view work, so it is worth being
precise about, and the precise answer is smaller than the impression.

**What is there:** a hard, bright, near-white horizontal line across the full
width of the frame, with a regular dashed or ticked pattern along it. Above it,
the lake and its shore; below it, the water column with fish.

**What it is not: two cameras.** Measured above and below the divider in
CnC_fishing —

    above-water shore     V 87    S 0.41
    below-water column    V 98    S 0.46

The underwater half is *not* darker and *not* desaturated. It is very slightly
brighter. Whatever separates the two halves, it is not a tint, an exposure
change or a second colour treatment.

**And the divider is not the waterline.** In CnC_underwater_2 the boat floats
roughly 75 px *below* the white line, which a boat on a surface cannot do. The
same dashed line, at the same weight and the same tick pattern, appears in all
three frames at different heights relative to the water. Read as a **UI overlay
— a casting-depth or distance gauge — drawn over both halves**, not as the
boundary between them. The actual boundary between above and below water is the
ordinary painted water surface, and the view across it is continuous.

**So the "split screen" in this reference is one continuous camera looking at a
world that happens to have water in it, plus a gauge drawn on top.** There is no
second viewport, no second camera and no per-pane treatment.

**What that is worth to us, stated carefully because it cuts both ways.** It
removes the assumption that a surface/underground split needs two cameras and
two render passes — the reference achieves its effect without either. But our
case is genuinely different in a way the reference cannot speak to: an
underground cave is *dark* and a lake is not, so our lower pane does need a
treatment the reference's lower half does not have. The reference has settled
the geometry question and has said nothing about the lighting one.

*Disproof:* the strongest counter-evidence would be a frame where content
visible above the line is inconsistent with content below it at the same x —
that would prove two views. Nothing in these three shows it; the far shore's
silhouette and the reflections run continuously across the boundary in
underwater_1. Video would settle it: if the two halves ever scroll at different
rates, it is two cameras and this entry is wrong.


ENTRY 6 — what the eye goes to when everything is moving.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: all eight.

The frames are nearly still, so this is the question the reference answers
worst, and the honest report is a partial one.

What can be said: in every frame the character and boat occupy a very small
fraction of the image — a few hundred pixels out of three million — and are
found immediately anyway. Three things do it, and none of them is size:

  - **Hue isolation.** The boat and figure are warm brown and cream in a frame
    that is otherwise entirely blue. They are the only warm object present.
  - **Value isolation.** In CnC_night the figure sits against the frame's
    brightest region (the moonlit water), not against the dark shore.
  - **The only hard edges in the frame.** Everything painted is soft-edged and
    dithered; the boat has a crisp outline.

The first of those is the one we have already adopted and the reason the player
is cool blue-grey against a warm world — the same trick, inverted, because our
world's temperature is the opposite of this one's. The third is free to us: our
player is a sprite against a cell grid.

*Disproof:* this needs footage, not stills. A still cannot show what the eye
tracks when a hundred things move, and that is precisely our case and not this
reference's. **Until someone watches gameplay of a game whose background
actually simulates — Noita is the obvious one — this question is open**, and the
three bullets above should be treated as hypotheses about a still image.


ENTRY 7 — the ground plane: what the water layer is actually doing, and the
          four mechanisms that make the frame read "into the page".
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_parallax_1.jpg, _2.jpg, _3.jpg. Measured 2026-08-16, Rec. 709
luminance, Pillow only, script kept out of the tree (throwaway).

This entry exists because the frame family was asked about directly. **It does
not admit anything** — the file's own bound says reference cannot admit a
roadmap item, and V19 was admitted by a request, not by these numbers. What is
below is what the request has to be built against.

**The frame is 7 layers, not 3.** Walking a single column (x=2900, the
unobstructed right side) and segmenting it into luminance plateaus gives five
bands above the horizon, then the plane, then a near silhouette:

    band              L      x band behind    x sky
    sky            173.6         -            1.00
    far range      129.2        0.74          0.74
    mid range      110.2        0.85          0.63
    near ridge      80.7        0.73          0.46
    shore treeline  63.5        0.79          0.37
    ground plane   77.5 -> 138.2  (see below)
    foreground rock 51.6        0.81          0.30

**Each band is about 0.78 of the band behind it** — geometric mean of the five
steps, none of them outside 0.73-0.85. Six silhouette steps, sky to nearest, is
a factor of 3.4. **Our mountains sit at 0.60 and the reference's same band
compounds to 0.63**, which is corroboration rather than agreement: ours was
picked from a luminance measurement against our own sky (entry 2), theirs falls
out of a ladder nobody involved had seen. Two independent routes to within 5%.

*This is a different measurement from entry 2's table and does not replace it.*
Entry 2 sampled max-channel V over hand-picked regions and found five bands;
this walks one column in luminance and finds seven. The two disagree on one
figure and it is worth naming: **entry 2 puts the foreground rock at 103 and
this puts it at 51.6.** Entry 2's region almost certainly included the plane
around the rock, since a near-black silhouette cannot sit above the treeline it
occludes. The monotonic descent both agree on is the finding; the rock's
absolute value should be read off this entry.

**The three frames share one value vocabulary.** The same fixed sample windows
return the same numbers in all three: sky 170.5 / 170.4 / 170.2, mid 81.4 /
80.7 / 80.5, near ridge 73.4 / 73.5 / 73.3 — inside 1%, worst band 3%. So the
ladder is a property of the style and not of one painting, which is the only
reason it is worth transferring at all.

**The plane occupies 47% of the frame and inverts the ladder.** The waterline
sits at y=766 of 1440 (median over columns 1500-2600). Below it the plane runs
L 77.5 at its far edge (#4d4c56, near-neutral) to L 138.2 at its near edge
(#768caa). So it climbs where every band above it descends.

Four mechanisms, and they are separable — this matters, because only some of
them survive the water being taken out:

  1. **The value ladder above the horizon.** 0.78 per band, compounding.
     *Transfers to land unchanged.*

  2. **The horizon is the darkest line in the frame.** Row-mean luminance falls
     monotonically from 156 at y=0 to a global minimum of 69 at y=768 — the
     waterline — then climbs to 140 by y=1200. The eye is given a dark pinch at
     the exact depth everything recedes toward, with both extremes bright.
     *Transfers, and is the mechanism most easily lost by accident.*

  3. **The plane's near edge carries the largest contrast in the frame.** Plane
     near edge 138.2 against foreground rock 51.6 is 87 levels. Every join
     further back is small: sky/far range 45, far/mid 19, mid/near 29,
     near/treeline 17, and the horizon join itself only 14. **Contrast grows
     with nearness and the frame spends most of it in the last two layers.**
     *Transfers.*

  4. **A texture gradient on the plane.** Mean horizontal distance between
     luminance reversals along a row, sampled x=300..2940: 13.0 px at y=860
     rising to 17.1 at y=1300 in frame 1, 12.6 -> 21.1 in frame 2, 10.6 -> 31.8
     in frame 3. Direction is consistent in all three; magnitude is 1.3x to
     3.0x. The plane's marks get wider toward the viewer. *Transfers, and is
     the one cue that needs no light source, no reflection and no colour.*

**What does NOT transfer is the reflection, and it is worth having measured
anyway.** Fitting L_below(d) = a * L_above(d/k) + b down columns 1500-2600
gives a best residual of 8.9 at k=0.6, a=0.442, b=+60.9, against residuals above
19 for every k >= 1.0. So the reflection is vertically compressed to about 0.6,
its contrast cut to 44%, and lifted +61 — and desaturated with it, S 0.24-0.30
below against 0.28-0.39 above. **It is a value-flattened restatement of the
stack, not a mirror copy.** Caveat the fit cannot resolve: a real water surface
also becomes less mirror-like toward the viewer, and that produces the same
numbers. For anything we would build the two are the same instruction anyway.

**The native art cell is about 10 px** on these 3440x1440 files (phase peak /
mean 1.393 at 10, next best 1.191 at 8), so the frames are roughly 344x144
native. Our viewport is 480x270 cells — **finer than the reference, not
coarser**, which means backdrop art authored at one art pixel per cell is
already less chunky than what these frames get their read from.

*Disproof, and one half of it is cheap.* The ladder rests on one column: sample
three more and if the step ratios spread outside 0.7-0.85 then "0.78 per band"
is an artifact of where the line was drawn. The plane mechanisms need video —
**if the plane's rows all scroll at one rate, mechanism 4 is decoration and any
per-row scrolling built for it is over-engineering.** That is the single
observation here most likely to be wrong in the expensive direction.


ENTRY 8 — the warm accent sits at a fixed value and crosses the depth ladder.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_parallax_1.jpg, CnC_parallax_3.jpg. Measured 2026-08-16.

Counting pixels with r > b per band, and taking the warm and cool populations'
mean luminance separately:

    band            warm%    warm L    cool L
    sky              0.0%       -       173.6
    far range        0.1%     103.5     129.0
    mid range        3.2%      96.2     101.9
    near ridge       6.1%      97.8      78.7
    shore treeline  47.2%      96.2      72.9
    plane, far      13.3%      98.6      84.7
    plane, near      5.0%     119.2     135.1
    foreground rock  9.3%      87.5      71.9

Two things, and the second is the useful one.

**The sky contains no warm pixels at all** — 0.0%, not 0.1%. The warm budget of
the entire frame is spent below the horizon line and mostly in one band: the
shore treeline is 47% warm and every other band is under 17%.

**The warm population sits at a near-constant luminance regardless of depth** —
96 to 104 in every band from the far range to the plane's far edge — while the
cool population descends 174, 129, 102, 79, 73. So the accent hue does not obey
the ladder; it **crosses** it. The same gold is brighter than its surroundings
in the near bands and darker than them in the far ones, which is why it reads
as one material lit by one light rather than as five different colours.

This is entry 6's hue-isolation finding at band scale rather than object scale,
and it is the part of it that does not need video to be trusted: entry 6 could
only say the boat is the frame's one warm object, and this says the whole style
allocates warmth as a fixed-value overlay across depths.

CnC_parallax_3 reproduces it (treeline 37.2% warm, warm L 96.0).

*Disproof:* CnC_lighthouse.jpg is the one frame in the set with a warm sky, so
it should break the 0.0% clause outright. **Run it there before treating any of
this as a rule rather than as a fact about the night frames.** A second, harder
disproof: if the warm L drifts with band once more frames are sampled, then this
is aerial perspective acting on gold like everything else and there is no
crossing.

ENTRY 9 — where the figure sits IN the plane, measured. CnC_lighthouse.jpg,
2026-08-16.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Observed because the tester named this frame specifically, as the thing our
player is supposed to be doing and is not. Entry 7 measured what the plane is
made of; this measures where the figure stands in it, which turns out to be
the part that was missing.

Column band x 200..900, avoiding the island. 1920x1080.

  horizon (brightest row of the middle band)   row  645   59.7% down
  hull waterline (densest dark row, x 700..960) row  920   85.2% down
  plane runs                                    645..1074  39.7% of the frame

  plane BEHIND the contact point   275 rows   64% of the plane
  plane IN FRONT of it             154 rows   36% of the plane

**The number to carry is that third.** The boat does not stand at the plane's
near edge looking across it; it is roughly two thirds of the way down, with a
full third of the plane between it and the viewer. That is what makes the
plane recede *around* the figure rather than behind it, and it is a fact about
composition, not about shading — no value, ramp or grade produces it.

The second fact, and it is the one that costs us a scene: **between the boat
and the horizon there is nothing but plane.** No silhouette, no clutter, no
vertical feature except the lighthouse island, which is deliberately off to one
side. The uninterrupted run from the figure to the horizon is what the eye
reads the distance along.

*How this transfers, since land is not water:* the third-in-front is the half
that transfers unchanged, because it is geometry. In our composition the plane
in front of the player is not backdrop at all — it is the simulated world, and
the player already stands on it. So the requirement lands on the *world*: the
terrain at spawn must present a flat, open surface running to where the
backdrop plane takes over, with the contact point around two thirds down the
combined band.

*Disproof, and it is a live one:* this is a single frame, and the figure is a
boat on water — a thing that cannot stand anywhere except in the plane, so the
composition may be forced rather than chosen. Check the 36% against
CnC_fishing.jpg before treating it as a ratio to author to. If it moves a lot,
what survives is the ordinal claim (the figure is inside the plane, not at its
near edge) and not the fraction.

*Disproof run, 2026-08-17, and it fired — partly.* CnC_fishing.jpg cannot
supply the ratio at all, and the reason is worth more than the number would
have been: **its plane does not run to the bottom of the frame.** The frame is
the split above/below-water device, cut by a hard white waterline at row ~683
(63% down), so the visible plane runs only from the far bank (~555) to that
cut. The boat's hull sits at ~645, which is 70% of *that* band. **That is not
the same measurement** — the lighthouse's plane is bounded by the frame edge
and this one by a graphic device — so the two fractions are not averageable and
64% does not become "64 and 70, call it 67".

What survives is exactly what this note predicted would survive: **the ordinal
claim.** Both frames put the contact well past the midpoint of the visible
plane, 64% and 70%, and neither is anywhere near 50%. Author to "clearly past
half, around two thirds", never to a specific fraction — there is no evidence
in this set that resolves finer than that, and a precise-looking number here
would be an accident of two incomparable crops.

*And the ordinal claim is the one that costs.* Even the loose version of it is
unreachable by any scene edit — `Camera::follow` centres strictly, so a
center-locked player cannot exceed ~50% of the band no matter what the terrain
does. See V22 in ROADMAP.md. **A reference reading that survives its own
disproof can still be unaffordable, and those are separate findings** — this
one was measured twice and is true, and the open question is whether a digging
game can pay for it at all.

ENTRY 10 — the subject moves, the composition does not.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Frames: CnC_fishing.jpg, CnC_underwater_1.jpg, CnC_underwater_2.jpg — the same
three as entry 5, read together instead of one at a time, which is the only
reason this is here.

Entry 9's disproof run had just established that the *fractions* in these frames
are not comparable — the fishing frame's plane is cut by its split-view
waterline rather than by the frame edge — and left only the ordinal claim
standing. That looked like the end of what the family could say. It was not,
because a fraction is not the only thing a set of frames can agree on.

Where the subject sits, top of frame to boat:

    CnC_fishing.jpg        ~0.60 down
    CnC_underwater_1.jpg   ~0.36 down
    CnC_underwater_2.jpg   ~0.27 down

Read as three attempts at one composition those numbers are a mess — a third of
the frame of disagreement, on the one element every frame is built around. Read
as three *states* they are not, and the thing that resolves them is what stays
still:

**The near volume holds the lower 55-65% of the frame in all three.** In the
fishing frame that volume is the lit plane the boat sits on and you look across
it. In the two underwater frames it is the water column and you look down into
it. The subject is not at an inconsistent height; it is riding on top of
whichever volume the frame is about, and it rises exactly when that volume goes
underneath it.

**So the rule is not "put the subject at height X". It is "give the volume the
frame is about the bottom two thirds, and let the subject sit on its edge."**
That is a rule about composition rather than about a coordinate, which is why
three frames that disagree about the coordinate can all obey it.

*What it was worth, immediately.* V22 had stalled on what looked like a genuine
conflict: the reference wants the player low so the ground plane gets its two
thirds, and digging wants them high so there is world visible below them to dig
into. Those are the same rule in two states — the volume worth showing changes
when you start digging, so the framing does too. It became V23, the camera's
vertical anchor, and the two anchors are read straight off the table above.

*Disproof, and it is a sharp one:* a frame where the subject sits low and the
volume of interest is nonetheless below them, or high with the interest above.
Either breaks the "sits on the edge of the volume" reading and leaves the three
heights as simple inconsistency. None of the eight frames shows it. **A moving
frame would test it far better than a still one** — this entry claims the
subject's height is a function of what the frame is about, and a video where the
camera never re-frames while the subject changes depth would disprove it
outright.

*And the caution entry 9 earned, restated because this entry is built on the
same three frames it was earned from:* a reading that survives its own disproof
can still be unaffordable, and those are separate findings. This one was
affordable — it cost a camera anchor and a state machine, both testable — but
that was luck about which item it landed in, not a property of the reading.

Observed because the owner asked for a comprehensive read of this frame with

layer infront of a background, the layers curently do not create the sense of

this measures the camera, the band extents, and what actually produces the
depth. Method: row-mean luminance (Rec. 709), saturation, saturation-weighted
circular-mean hue, and **16x16-tile standard deviation as "detail energy"**,
sampled in a 30-70% centre corridor where the vignette would otherwise dominate.

The waterline is at row 719 and the hunter's head sits just below it. A figure
whose head falls below the horizon is one the lens is level with - this is a

*2. Eight bands, and three of them we have no equivalent for.*

  8 cool upper sky    0-12%     lum  83   the frame's cool anchor
  7 sunset sky       12-50%    lum 122-180  brightest thing in the frame
  6 mountain ridge   43-58%    lum  83   flat, hazed toward the sky

**Below the sky the reference divides ~50 points of screen height among four

not - bands 9-15 are 1.0-2.2% solid - so **the trigger arguably has fired, and
that is an item to be argued, not a thing to go and build.**

*3. The six mechanisms, in order of how much depth they carry.*

  (a) DETAIL ENERGY, and it is the strongest cue in the frame. Local texture
      variance climbs monotonically toward the camera: far sky 2.24, treeline
      10.5, near water 20.0, foreground 27.6 - a 12x range. **It is independent
      of luminance, so it survives any night grade untouched.** Ours, measured on
      assets/backdrop_ground.bmp: 0.00 -> 8.85 at 60% through the tile -> 5.10 at
      the near edge. A fifth of the range and running backwards over the last
      third. V22 part 4 moved it to 7.8 mean but did not fix the direction.

  (b) A COOL PINCH AT THE HORIZON. Warmth (R-B) is double-peaked: sky +125,
      horizon -14, water +90, foreground -6. The horizon is where colour drains
      out, and warmth returns on both sides of it.

  (c) THE PLANE RETURNS THE SKY. The water reflects the sky's hue and the sun
      path, so top and bottom halves share one palette - 72% of the sky's warmth
      comes back in the plane. **This is the single strongest cohesion device in
      the image and it is not parallax**; it is the ground being made of the same
      light as the sky. We have no equivalent and no item for one.

  (d) THE NEAR FIELD FRAMES, IT DOES NOT FLOOR. Edge-to-centre luminance ratio
      bottoms at 0.21, and the dark mass runs up both edges and across the top as
      well as the bottom. ENTRY 12 part 2 said do not build it as a bottom band
      only; this is that, measured.

  (e) FLAT MASSES, CONTRAST AT THE EDGES. Bands carry little internal structure;
      the contrast budget is spent on the boundary between them. This is ENTRY 3
      re-measured and it holds.

  (f) DARK FIGURE ON A BRIGHT PLANE. Figure luminance 51.1 against surrounding
      plane 115.4 - the plane is 2.26x brighter than the thing standing in it.

*4. How the figure is tied to the ground, at pixel scale, and none of it is a
value.* Four devices: no hard contact edge (the legs dissolve into a slightly
darker horizontal smear, no outline); a broken reflection of a few dark dashes
